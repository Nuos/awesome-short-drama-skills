Round two: choosing a premise

The brief asks for the premise of a new revenge-fantasy story (the "satisfying payback" genre big on Chinese web novel sites and vertical drama apps) that could later become a web novel or a vertical short drama. Only the premise: a one-line pitch and a premise note. No genre, length or platform. The entries were 11 skills, picked by stars from those in the short drama and novel lists that can come up with a premise from scratch, plus a control with no skill, all on Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies. When a skill offered several options, it was told to pick the one it recommends most.

Reviewers could not see who wrote which premise. Each premise got a story card, to see how alike they are. Then all 12 entries were compared in pairs, run n against run n, with the order swapped each time; an entry only wins if it wins both orders. A win scores 1 point and a tie 0.5, each entry has 33 comparisons, and entries are ranked by points. Only the final recommended premise counts, judged on five things: the pitch, freshness, conflict and payoffs, staying power, and feasibility. Length and market analysis earn nothing. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts.

Findings

  1. Both reviewers put the same four skills on top, in a different order. Opus ranked Tomsawyerhu/Chinese-WebNovel-Skill, webnovel-writer, bookflow-skill and ai-short-drama; GPT ranked bookflow-skill, ai-short-drama, webnovel-writer and Tomsawyerhu/Chinese-WebNovel-Skill. Each has its strength. Tomsawyerhu gave its heroines rare legal jobs and the most gripping pitches: in one, thirteen years after a rich family drove her father to his death, a court lottery sends her to seize everything they own. The law needs checking, though. webnovel-writer gave its powers rules with a price, such as collecting only debts that are truly owed, and paying back double for taking too much. bookflow-skill tests an idea with a short story first, with few locations and a small cast, so it is the easiest to produce. ai-short-drama's supernatural powers all follow rules, which makes its premises fresh, but all three need effects or AI imagery.
  2. Most skills chose better premises than no skill, but not all. The no-skill control ranked 7th with Opus (6 skills scored higher) and 10th with GPT (9 scored higher). Its one-line pitches were direct, but it was weak on freshness and staying power, and every one of its three premises had a skill writing the same kind of story.
  3. The bottom two both start by checking the charts. Both reviewers put oh-story and chinese-longnovel-skill last. Both want to read the bestseller charts of Chinese web novel platforms before choosing a premise; there was no network this round, and both said in their drafts that they had no chart data. They lost points for the premises themselves, not for missing data (market analysis earned nothing): reviewers kept calling them familiar formulas, like a reborn hero who knows the future or an appraiser who spots treasures. How they do with the network, this round cannot say.
  4. Lots of premises collided. The 36 premises fell into 25 story types. Five titles start with "Cooling-Off Period", and 18 story cards mention settling accounts, written by all 12 entries.
  5. The model's default names are still there. 11 of 36 protagonists have the surname Xu, and a character named Zhou Yan appears in the drafts of 4 entries, the control among them.
  6. No skill stopped to let you pick a direction. All 36 runs wrote the premise to a file in their first turn.

Round one: an open brief

The brief says only "write a vertical short drama", plus the length (60 episodes of 1–2 minutes) and three deliverables: a character bible, a 60-episode outline with paywall points, and full scripts for episodes 1–3. No genre, characters or plot. Every run used Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies, so the only variable is which skill was installed. Scripts that ship with a skill could run inside a system sandbox.

The review had two parts, and neither could see which skill wrote what. First, each draft got a story card, to see how alike the stories are. Then run n of each skill was compared with run n of the control, once for the scripts and once for the characters and outline. Each comparison was repeated with the order swapped, and a skill only counts as winning if it won both times. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) then re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts, and results where the two reviewers disagree are marked as disputed.

Findings

  1. With a skill, scripts were mostly easier to shoot. In the script comparisons, 9 of 10 skills beat the control in most runs, and 5 won all three. They won on shootability, dialogue and AI tells: the control often packed four to eight scenes and flashbacks into an episode and ran past two minutes. The control's episode-ending hooks, though, were often stronger. shuohao-skills was the only one to lose all three, but the GPT re-judge gave it two wins and a tie, so that result is disputed. Both reviewers found 9 of 10 skills beating the control in most runs; GPT just named short-drama-factory as the one that did not.
  2. Characters and outlines split the field more. ai-short-drama, Shanyin Screenwriting Master, screenwriting-skills, zenstory and manju-laoli won all three runs, and Emma won two and tied one. qianqianwriting, hongguo, short-drama-factory and shuohao won at most one of three, mostly for formulaic premises and overstuffed episodes; the GPT re-judge agreed only on shuohao, so the other three are disputed. All 33 drafts put the first paywall at episode 10.
  3. Skills do change which story gets written. The 33 dramas fell into 21 story types. Two runs of the same skill landed in the same type 12% of the time; two runs of different skills, only 2.4%. hongguo, for example, wrote a daughter-in-law settling accounts with her in-laws all three times, and ai-short-drama wrote two dramas called The Old Brine.
  4. The model's defaults show up whichever skill is installed. 15 of the 33 drafts have a character named Xiaoman, written by 10 of the 11 entries, and 10 heroines have the surname Shen. zenstory and shuohao each wrote a drama called The Appraiser whose heroine is chief appraiser at the same fictional auction house. Distinctive names and premises still have to come from a person.
  5. Most skills do not ask you to pick a direction first. Across 33 runs, only a few runs of manju-laoli, Shanyin and shuohao stopped to offer several story options.
  6. Everything needs cleanup before it goes to a crew. Every skill's drafts carry IDs, runtime estimates, checkpoints or self-check tables mixed into the text.

Limits of this round

  • One model. The results only describe these skills under claude-opus-5-5; another model may differ.
  • Most skills did not ask for a direction. So the differences between runs come mostly from the model's own randomness. A real user's choice at that step might make the story more distinctive.
  • No network. For safety, the agent had no network and could not read files outside its own folder; skills that would check the charts had to rely on what they ship with.
  • The reviewers are AI. Story cards, story types and comparisons were done by claude-opus-5-5, the same model that wrote the drafts. Re-judged by GPT, single comparisons agreed 79% of the time, but the per-pair results (a win needs both orders) agreed on only 42 of 60 pairs, so check whether a skill's result is disputed. 88% of the quotes in Opus's reasoning can be found in the drafts, and 96% of GPT's. Details are in the reviewer health check (in Chinese). Next round we plan to add human screenwriters.

How we test

  1. The same input, as little as possible: the brief gives only the length and deliverables, with no genre, characters or plot, and every skill in the round shares it.
  2. The skill is the only variable: the same agent, model, reasoning effort and tool permissions, with versions recorded. The control just has no skill.
  3. Pinned skill versions: the commit of each repo at test time is recorded.
  4. Each skill's own process: the run opens by calling the entry skill. When a skill stops to ask, it gets the same fixed reply, and everything else follows the skill's own recommendation. If it offered several genres or story options, in round one run n picked option n, so the three runs would tell different stories; round two compares the choosing itself, so there it picks the option it recommends most.
  5. Skill scripts can run: commands run in a system sandbox with no network and no view of other entries' output.
  6. Three runs each, all kept: no cherry-picking.
  7. Blind review: reviewers do not know which skill wrote a draft, and every comparison is run in both orders.

What we look at

DimensionWhat it means
HooksDo the opening and each episode ending make you want the next one
DialogueDoes it sound like people talking, and do characters sound different
AI tellsHow much boilerplate, parallelism and empty adjectives
ShootabilityIs the runtime right, are the scenes and cast realistic, how much rework before shooting
CharactersAre motives clear, do characters have flaws and change, are side characters more than tools
FreshnessAre the premise and set pieces fresh or the usual formula
StructureDoes conflict escalate, do the twists hold, are there long sags
Paywall pointsAre they well placed, with enough of a hook before each

Round two compares premises on five things: the pitch (does the one-liner make you want it now), freshness (is it a familiar formula with new names), conflict and payoffs (what the hero wants, who stands in the way, can the payoffs keep coming), staying power (does it hold up for the length it plans), and feasibility (can it be written and filmed, any obvious review risk).

Reviews give observations and examples. Round one has no overall scores; round two compares everyone with everyone and ranks by points. The runner is evals/run.py and the reviewer is evals/judge.py, so anyone can reproduce the method.
