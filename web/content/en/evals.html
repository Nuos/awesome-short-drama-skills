Round two: choosing a premise

The brief asks for the premise of a new revenge-fantasy story (the "satisfying payback" genre big on Chinese web novel sites and vertical drama apps) that could later become a web novel or a vertical short drama. Only the premise: a one-line pitch and a premise note. No genre, length or platform. The entries were 11 skills, picked by stars from those in the short drama and novel lists that can come up with a premise from scratch, plus a control with no skill, all on Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies. When a skill offered several options, it was told to pick the one it recommends most.

Reviewers could not see who wrote which premise. Each premise got a story card, to see how alike they are. Then all 12 entries were compared in pairs, run n against run n, with the order swapped each time; an entry only wins if it wins both orders. A win scores 1 point and a tie 0.5, each entry has 33 comparisons, and entries are ranked by points. Only the final recommended premise counts, judged on five things: the pitch, freshness, conflict and payoffs, staying power, and how it plays on screen. Length and market analysis earn nothing. Reviewers judge as if AI will make the whole thing: effects, powers, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts lose no points, and big spectacle earns none. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts.

Findings

  1. Both reviewers put the same three skills on top, in a different order. Opus ranked Tomsawyerhu/Chinese-WebNovel-Skill, webnovel-writer and ai-short-drama; GPT ranked ai-short-drama, Tomsawyerhu/Chinese-WebNovel-Skill and webnovel-writer. All three beat the control in all three runs. Each has its strength. Tomsawyerhu gave its heroines rare legal jobs and the most gripping pitches, with every payoff landing on an opponent's face: in one, the court-appointed receiver sent to seize a family's assets is the woman they ruined thirteen years earlier. webnovel-writer gave its powers rules with a price, such as "collect only real debts; take a cent too much and it backfires", though collecting debt after debt gets repetitive mid-story. ai-short-drama was the freshest, with powers that follow rules, but the rules leave its heroes too passive and the payoffs come slowly.
  2. Most skills chose better premises than no skill, but not all. Both reviewers ranked the no-skill control 9th, with 8 skills above it. Its one-line pitches were direct, but it was weak on conflict, staying power and how it plays on screen, often paying off through legal explanations or off camera; every one of its three premises had a skill writing the same kind of story.
  3. Both reviewers agree on the bottom three. They are chinese-longnovel-skill, Distilled-Novel-Toolbox and oh-story. oh-story and chinese-longnovel-skill both want to read the bestseller charts of Chinese web novel platforms before choosing a premise; there was no network this round, and both said in their drafts that they had no chart data. They lost points for the premises themselves, not for missing data (market analysis earns nothing): familiar formulas, with key information living in the hero's head where no face can show it. How they choose with network access, this round cannot tell.
  4. The middle of the table is where the reviewers differ. 0xsline/short-drama, sumeru, bookflow-skill and webfiction-write each moved three places or more between the two rankings. bookflow-skill, for example, tests a premise with a 10,000–20,000-character short story first; Opus thought that could not carry a 60–70-episode drama, and GPT did not.
  5. Lots of premises collided. The 36 premises fell into 25 story types. Five titles start with "Cooling-Off Period", and 18 story cards mention settling accounts, written by all 12 entries.
  6. The model's default names are still there. 11 of 36 protagonists have the surname Xu, and a character named Zhou Yan appears in the drafts of 4 entries, the control among them.
  7. No skill stopped to let you pick a direction. All 36 runs wrote the premise to a file in their first turn.

Round one: an open brief

The brief says only "write a vertical short drama", plus the length (60 episodes of 1–2 minutes) and three deliverables: a character bible, a 60-episode outline with paywall points, and full scripts for episodes 1–3. No genre, characters or plot. Every run used Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies, so the only variable is which skill was installed. Scripts that ship with a skill could run inside a system sandbox.

The review had two parts, and neither could see which skill wrote what. First, each draft got a story card, to see how alike the stories are. Then run n of each skill was compared with run n of the control, once for the scripts and once for the characters and outline. Each comparison was repeated with the order swapped, and a skill only counts as winning if it won both times. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) then re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts, and results where the two reviewers disagree are marked as disputed. Reviewers judge as if AI will make the whole thing: effects, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts lose no points, and big spectacle earns none.

Findings

  1. Scripts: four skills beat the control in all three runs, and both reviewers agree. drama-skills, Shanyin Screenwriting Master, screenwriting-skills and Emma won all three runs under both Opus and GPT. They won on dialogue, AI tells and how well the script plays on screen: short lines, characters who sound different, emotion carried by actions and objects, and episodes that fit in one to two minutes. The control crammed too much into each episode, often ran well past two minutes, and explained key deductions in voice-over. On the other six, the reviewers split: Opus found none of them beating the control in most runs, while GPT found all of them did except short-drama-factory.
  2. Characters and outlines: five skills won all three runs, and both reviewers agree. The same four plus ai-short-drama. They won on characters: side characters with their own motives and moves, and a lead whose flaws cost her something real. Skills had no edge on paywall points, and all 33 drafts put the first paywall at episode 10. Both reviewers gave shuohao two losses and a tie; the other four are disputed.
  3. Skills do change which story gets written. The 33 dramas fell into 21 story types. Two runs of the same skill landed in the same type 12% of the time; two runs of different skills, only 2.4%. hongguo, for example, wrote a daughter-in-law settling accounts with her in-laws all three times, and ai-short-drama wrote two dramas called The Old Brine.
  4. The model's defaults show up whichever skill is installed. 15 of the 33 drafts have a character named Xiaoman, written by 10 of the 11 entries, and 10 heroines have the surname Shen. zenstory and shuohao each wrote a drama called The Appraiser whose heroine is chief appraiser at the same fictional auction house. Distinctive names and premises still have to come from a person.
  5. Most skills do not ask you to pick a direction first. Across 33 runs, only a few runs of manju-laoli, Shanyin and shuohao stopped to offer several story options.
  6. Everything needs cleanup before it goes to AI production. Every skill's drafts carry IDs, runtime estimates, checkpoints or self-check tables mixed into the text.

Limits of this round

  • One model. The results only describe these skills under claude-opus-5-5; another model may differ.

How we test

  1. The same input, as little as possible: the brief gives only the length and deliverables, with no genre, characters or plot, and every skill in the round shares it.
  2. The skill is the only variable: the same agent, model, reasoning effort and tool permissions, with versions recorded. The control just has no skill.
  3. Pinned skill versions: the commit of each repo at test time is recorded.
  4. Each skill's own process: the run opens by calling the entry skill. When a skill stops to ask, it gets the same fixed reply, and everything else follows the skill's own recommendation. If it offered several genres or story options, in round one run n picked option n, so the three runs would tell different stories; round two compares the choosing itself, so there it picks the option it recommends most.
  5. Skill scripts can run: commands run in a system sandbox with no network and no view of other entries' output.
  6. Three runs each, all kept: no cherry-picking.
  7. Blind review: reviewers do not know which skill wrote a draft, and every comparison is run in both orders.
  8. Judged as AI-made film: reviewers assume the draft goes to AI to be made start to finish, with no actors or sets. Effects, powers, big set pieces, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts neither lose nor earn points; what counts is whether viewers would keep watching.

What we look at

DimensionWhat it means
HooksDo the opening and each episode ending make you want the next one
DialogueDoes it sound like people talking, and do characters sound different
AI tellsHow much boilerplate, parallelism and empty adjectives
On screenDoes each 1–2 minute episode fit (judged by images and lines, not by counting scenes or cast); do emotions and payoffs land on faces and actions rather than inner monologue and long narration; can it go straight to AI production
CharactersAre motives clear, do characters have flaws and change, are side characters more than tools
FreshnessAre the premise and set pieces fresh or the usual formula
StructureDoes conflict escalate, do the twists hold, are there long sags
Paywall pointsAre they well placed, with enough of a hook before each

Round two compares premises on five things: the pitch (does the one-liner make you want it now), freshness (is it a familiar formula with new names), conflict and payoffs (what the hero wants, who stands in the way, can the payoffs keep coming), staying power (does it hold up for the length it plans), and on screen (who the audience rides with and whether that person has a face that can act; whose face each payoff lands on; whether key emotions lean on inner monologue).

Reviews give observations and examples. Round one has no overall scores; round two compares everyone with everyone and ranks by points. The runner is evals/run.py and the reviewer is evals/judge.py, so anyone can reproduce the method.
